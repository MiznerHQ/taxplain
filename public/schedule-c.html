What this is

Schedule C (officially "Profit or Loss from Business") is the IRS form you use to report income and expenses from a business you run as a sole proprietor or a single-member LLC that hasn't elected corporate tax treatment. It's attached to your Form 1040 every year you're self-employed.

Unlike a W-2 job, self-employment doesn't come with taxes withheld automatically. Schedule C is where you calculate your actual net profit — total business income minus deductible business expenses — and that number is what the IRS taxes, not your gross revenue.

If you freelance, drive for a rideshare app, sell products online, consult, or run any kind of one-person business, Schedule C is likely part of your tax return, even if you also have a regular job on the side.

You can file more than one Schedule C if you run multiple, distinct businesses — each one gets its own form, with its own income and expense totals. If your side work is more of a hobby without a real intent to turn a profit, different rules apply, and it's worth discussing that distinction with a tax professional before assuming Schedule C is the right form.

Who it affects
✓ Applies to

Sole proprietors, freelancers, independent contractors, and single-member LLCs with business income during the tax year.

↑ Also applies to

Side-hustle income — even part-time gig work or occasional freelance projects generally require a Schedule C if you're operating with intent to profit.

📅 SELF-EMPLOYMENT TAX THRESHOLD

If your net profit on Schedule C is $400 or more, you generally owe self-employment tax in addition to income tax, calculated on a separate form (Schedule SE).

Breakdown

What goes on Schedule C

The form walks through your business activity in a few main sections. Getting each part right matters — it directly affects both your income tax and your self-employment tax. Most filers work through it once a year, but keeping running totals throughout the year makes the process much faster and far less prone to missed deductions.

  • Gross income — total money your business brought in before any expenses.
  • Cost of goods sold — if you sell physical products, this covers what it cost you to produce or acquire them.
  • Business expenses — a long list of categories like advertising, supplies, insurance, vehicle use, and contract labor.
  • Home office deduction — if you use part of your home regularly and exclusively for business.
  • Net profit or loss — the bottom-line number, which flows to Schedule 1 and then your Form 1040.
  • Depreciation — for larger business assets like equipment or vehicles, spread out over several years rather than deducted all at once.
Schedule C vs. 1099-NEC

This is the single most common point of confusion around Schedule C — and it makes sense, since the two forms are closely related but do very different jobs.

1099-NEC

A form a client or platform sends you, reporting how much they paid you during the year. It's informational — it doesn't calculate anything or get "filed" by you.

Schedule C

The form you file, combining all your business income (including 1099-NEC amounts, cash payments, and anything else) with your expenses to calculate what you actually owe tax on.

Getting a 1099-NEC doesn't automatically mean you file Schedule C — but if that income came from self-employment work, it almost always does. And the reverse matters too: you still owe tax on self-employment income even if a client never sent you a 1099-NEC at all, which is increasingly common now that the reporting threshold has risen from $600 to $2,000 for payments made in 2026.

Related forms often involved

Schedule C rarely stands alone. Depending on your business, several other forms typically come along with it.

  • Schedule SE — calculates your self-employment tax based on your Schedule C net profit.
  • Form 1040-ES — used to pay estimated quarterly taxes, since self-employment income has no withholding.
  • Form 8829 — the detailed calculation form for the home office deduction, if you're claiming one.
Common mistakes to avoid
⚠️ Reporting only 1099-NEC income

Some filers assume Schedule C income equals their 1099-NEC totals. If clients paid you in cash, check, or through platforms without issuing a form, that income is still taxable and still belongs on Schedule C.

⚠️ Skipping the self-employment tax

Net profit over $400 triggers self-employment tax whether or not you owe regular income tax that year. Forgetting Schedule SE is one of the most common and costly Schedule C errors.

A third common issue: mixing personal and business expenses in the same account. It makes Schedule C far harder to prepare accurately, and it's one of the first things that draws scrutiny if your return is ever reviewed. Separate accounts, even simple ones, save real time and reduce errors.

What to do right now
Gather every source of business income for the year — not just 1099-NECs — along with receipts or records for business expenses. If you haven't been tracking these separately from personal spending, start now; it makes filing Schedule C and calculating self-employment tax far more accurate.

Frequently asked questions
What is Schedule C used for?
Schedule C is used to report the profit or loss from a business you operate as a sole proprietor or single-member LLC. It calculates your net business income, which then flows to Form 1040 and is used to figure your self-employment tax.
Do I need Schedule C if I got a 1099-NEC?
Usually yes. A 1099-NEC just reports income paid to you; it doesn't report your expenses or calculate your tax. If that income came from self-employment or freelance work, you typically report it on Schedule C so you can deduct business expenses against it.
What's the difference between Schedule C and 1099-NEC?
Form 1099-NEC is an information return a client sends you showing how much they paid you. Schedule C is the form you file to report your total business income and expenses, including amounts from any 1099-NECs you received, plus cash or other payments.
How do I fill out Schedule C?
You'll report your business's gross income, subtract your business expenses in the listed categories (like supplies, advertising, and home office costs), and the result is your net profit or loss, which carries over to Schedule 1 and Form 1040.
Do I owe self-employment tax on Schedule C income?
If your net profit from Schedule C is $400 or more, you generally owe self-employment tax, which covers Social Security and Medicare. This is calculated separately on Schedule SE and is in addition to regular income tax.
